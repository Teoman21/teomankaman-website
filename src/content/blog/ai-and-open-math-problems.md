In recent months, Levent Alpöge has [claimed](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) that he proved the Riemann Hypothesis and [disproved](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/ai-disproves-a-decades-old-mathematical-idea-the-biggest-conjecture-that-the-tech-has-played-a-role-in-yet-180989189/) the Jacobian conjecture with the help of Claude. This is very interesting, because we can see AI getting better and better, and now we can suddenly solve problems that have been open for over a century. So which one is next? I was talking to my friends and professor about this: will AI finally solve P vs NP, and what would that mean for us? I don't know, but I think we need to be ready, because it could happen any time now.

Or does the universe have some kind of fundamental limit on which problems can be solved? We actually know some limits exist: Gödel showed there are true statements no proof system can prove, and Turing showed some problems can't be solved by any computer. But I don't believe the famous open problems are like that. I think they can be solved with enough thought. The real question is whether "enough thought" is something we can actually afford, and I come back to that below.

## Is math dead now?

I don't think math could ever be dead, but I feel like we are living in a time similar to the discovery of quantum mechanics, when the foundations of a field suddenly shift. Part of what makes this moment different is that AI proofs can now be checked by proof assistants like Lean, so we can trust a result without a human verifying every line. In the next 10 years, I think we will discover groundbreaking theories and proofs this way. But humans are still needed. Even the Riemann Hypothesis proof took months of guidance from an expert mathematician, and I don't believe AI can solve these problems without that kind of human expertise.

## So is AI smarter than us?

This is a very interesting question, and I believe it has a lot of depth. My professor [believes](https://davidbau.com/archives/2026/11/05/one_unthinkable_thought.html) AI will never be beyond human understanding. His argument comes from Turing machines: a human with a pencil, unlimited paper, and unlimited time can compute anything a computer can, so given enough time, a human could understand anything an AI does. I agree this is true in theory. But in practice, "enough time" might mean longer than a lifetime, or even generations, and at that point the claim becomes impossible to test. We can never actually run the experiment that shows a human reaching the same understanding. So if it takes us decades to understand what an AI figured out in hours, then for every real decision we face, the AI is the only one who understands the problem in time. In that practical sense, I think AI can be smarter than us, even if it isn't in theory.

This is the same idea as the math question. In principle, most problems can be solved and most ideas can be understood. In practice, time is the limit. And if the gap between AI and humans is practical rather than fundamental, then it's something we can work on. That's why I care about understanding what happens inside trained models: interpretability is how we make what AI learns understandable to us faster, before the gap grows too large to close.
